# Microplastics in Human Brain Tissue: What Has Been Reported and How Much Weight It Bears

## Executive Summary

Multiple independent laboratories, using different analytical methods, have now reported synthetic and semi-synthetic polymer particles in human brain tissue. That qualitative finding is reasonably secure. The quantitative claims built on top of it — that the brain carries 3,000–5,000 µg of plastic per gram of tissue, 7–30 times the burden of liver or kidney, rising roughly 50% in eight years, and 26,000 µg/g in dementia — rest almost entirely on one laboratory's pyrolysis gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) data, and those data are now facing a quantified interference mechanism that could inflate them by orders of magnitude.

The central problem is specific and testable. Brain is a lipid-rich matrix (roughly 60% fat by dry weight, against under 5% for liver and kidney), and the dominant polymer reported in brain is polyethylene, at about 75% of polymer mass [1][2]. A peer-reviewed validation study showed that triacylglycerols — ordinary fats — pyrolyze into the same marker products used to quantify polyethylene, and that in lipid-rich food matrices this inflated apparent polyethylene by roughly 100–450-fold until an enzymatic clean-up was added [3]. Whether that factor transfers to brain is not established, but it is the single most important open question in this literature.

**Bottom line:** treat "polymers are present in brain tissue" as supported by several groups and methods; treat the absolute mass concentrations, the liver/kidney ranking, the 2016→2024 trend, and the dementia association as provisional, single-laboratory results whose magnitude is contested on mechanistic grounds.

## Key Findings

1. **The load-bearing study is Nihart et al., Nature Medicine 2025.** It sampled frontal cortex only, from University of New Mexico autopsy cases in 2016 and 2024 (n = 20–28 per timepoint), 28 East Coast brain-bank samples (deaths 1997–2013), and 12 dementia decedents [1]. It reported median brain burdens of 3,345 µg/g (2016) and 4,917 µg/g (2024), against 433 µg/g liver and 404 µg/g kidney in 2024 [1].

2. **The reported brain values are extraordinarily high in absolute terms.** At 4,917 µg/g, roughly 0.5% of brain wet weight would be plastic. Independent scientists quoted in coverage noted this exceeds concentrations reported in sewage sludge, which they called implausible [4].

3. **Polyethylene dominance is the crux of the dispute.** Polyethylene was about 75% of brain polymer mass versus 44–57% in liver and kidney [1]. A peer-reviewed Matters Arising argues that this uniformity across organs, times and individuals is unlike environmental exposure patterns and is consistent with a matrix artifact [2].

4. **A quantified interference mechanism exists.** Rauert et al. (2022) showed that saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated triacylglycerols produce the same polyethylene pyrolysis products (C10–C14 alkenes, C21 diene) with matching retention times and ion ratios [3]. Apparent polyethylene in milk, egg and lamb fell from 12,400–32,400 µg/g to 72–240 µg/g after enzymatic digestion and solvent washes — a 100–450-fold overestimation, calculated from the reported before-and-after values [3].

5. **Contamination and validation controls were reportedly thin in the Nihart study.** The Matters Arising cites absent procedural and field blanks, no matrix-contamination assessment, digestion efficiency below 90% in several brain samples, no spike-recovery or limit-of-detection validation, and no diagnostic marker ions [2]. The authors' published reply is paywalled and its substantive defense could not be verified [2].

6. **Independent detection exists but does not replicate the magnitudes.** A 2026 Nature Health study by a separate Chinese group found micro/nanoplastics in 99.4% of 156 diseased brain samples and 100% of 35 healthy samples using five complementary methods, with airborne controls reading zero — but its absolute concentrations are behind a paywall [5]. A single-donor O-PTIR study found 24.4 fibers per gram of white matter [6], and an unrefereed preprint reports electron-microscopy remnants in choroid plexus and subventricular tissue without quantification [7].

7. **Interlaboratory reproducibility is poor even on clean reference materials.** A 56-laboratory VAMAS comparison found reproducibility standard deviations of 46–129% on polyethylene and PET reference tablets, no correlation with method, instrument settings or experience, and non-target polymers in sample tablets attributed to contamination [8].

8. **The broader human-tissue literature shares the weakness.** A PRISMA review of 218 studies (2000–2024) found few consistently implemented blank controls or spike-recovery experiments [9].

9. **The dementia signal is the least secure claim of all.** The 26,076 µg/g median in 12 dementia decedents [1] has no independent replication in the retrieved literature, and the one directly relevant nanoplastic-in-Alzheimer's preprint could not be retrieved.

## Detailed Analysis

### 1. What has actually been reported, and in which tissue

The evidence base is narrow and dominated by one group. The table below summarizes the studies that report polymer detection in brain tissue, with the caveat that the metrics are not commensurable: Py-GC/MS yields polymer mass per gram, while infrared and Raman methods yield particle counts per gram.

| Study | Tissue and region | Donors | Method | Reported result | Contamination controls |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nihart et al., Nat Med 2025 [1] | Frontal cortex; liver, kidney comparators | 20–28 per timepoint (2016, 2024); 28 brain-bank; 12 dementia | Py-GC/MS, ATR-FTIR, SEM/TEM-EDS | 3,345–4,917 µg/g brain; 433/404 µg/g liver/kidney; 26,076 µg/g dementia | Reportedly absent per critique [2] |
| Li et al., Nature Health 2026 [5] | Tumour and healthy brain | 113 tumour patients; 5 post-mortem donors | LDIR, Py-GC/MS, nanoscale Py-GC/MS, O-PTIR, SEM | 99.4% and 100% detection; concentrations paywalled | Airborne, intraoperative, glove-rinsate blanks |
| Luboński et al., PMC12454625 2025 [6] | Brain white matter; six other organs | 1 (24-year-old male) | O-PTIR, SEM-EDS, DLS, MALDI-TOF | 24.4 fibers/g; PET, cellulose, polyimide, PLA | Not reported |
| Amato-Lourenço et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024 [10] | Olfactory bulb | 15 decedents (median 69.5 y) | µFTIR | 8/15 positive; 16 particles; PP 43.8%; 5.5–26.4 µm | Not reported in accessible summaries |
| Choroid plexus preprint, Research Square 2026 [7] | Choroid plexus, subventricular tissue, CSF leukocytes | 5 + 5 + 6 | TEM/EDX | Remnants in all cases; no quantification | Not reported |
| Environmental Pollution pilot 2026 [11] | Brain, plasma, CSF | Not stated in abstract | Py-GC/MS (11 polymers), LDIR | PE and PVC dominant; brain > plasma/CSF | Not stated in abstract |

Two features of this table matter more than any single row. First, only the Nihart study reports mass concentrations alongside same-study liver and kidney comparators, so the widely repeated "brain is 7–30 times liver and kidney" claim has exactly one source [1]. Second, the independent studies that do exist measure different things — particle counts, detection rates, morphological remnants — and therefore cannot confirm or refute a mass figure even in principle.

The olfactory bulb study deserves separate mention because it is often cited as independent confirmation. It found 16 particles and fibers across 8 of 15 decedents, with polypropylene the most common polymer at 43.8% and particle sizes of 5.5–26.4 µm [10]. That polymer profile is notably different from the polyethylene-dominated cortex findings, and the method cannot detect below about 3 µm or assess nanoplastics at all. These details come from secondary summaries; the primary paper's blank and detection-limit data were not accessible.

### 2. Concentrations, sizes, and the problem of incommensurable metrics

The Nihart concentrations are mass-normalized and derived from a single ~500 mg formalin-fixed tissue sample digested in 10% KOH, ultracentrifuged, and the pellet analyzed against a 12-polymer standard [12]. The reported distribution is wide: 2016 median 3,345 µg/g (IQR 1,267–5,213) and 2024 median 4,917 µg/g (IQR 4,026–5,608), with the East Coast brain-bank samples at 1,254 µg/g [1]. The authors reported no association with age (P = 0.87), sex, race/ethnicity or cause of death, and a significant 2016→2024 increase (P = 0.01) [1].

Particle size data are far thinner than the mass data. Transmission electron microscopy of resuspended brain pellets showed largely 100–200 nm shards and flakes, and scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy confirmed carbon-based, non-mineral particles in situ in only two subjects; imaging covered a small subset (n = 10 normal, n = 3 dementia) [1]. Light microscopy cannot resolve below 5 µm, and the authors reported no 1–5 µm refractile inclusions in brain, in contrast to liver and kidney [1].

This creates a coherence problem that the critique literature emphasizes. Particles in the 100–200 nm range are below the detection limits of most infrared and Raman tissue methods — micro-FTIR limits are typically 10–20 µm and micro-Raman 0.5–5 µm [8], with several reviewed tissue studies reporting limits above 20 µm [13]. So the very size class that would need to dominate a nanoplastic mass signal is largely invisible to the confirmatory spectroscopic methods, while Py-GC/MS sees mass but no size or shape at all [13].

Comparisons across tissues compound the difficulty. The Nihart paper places brain far above placenta (median 63.4 µg/g) and testes (median 299 µg/g) [1], but the single-donor O-PTIR study found thyroid (40.4 particles/g) exceeding brain (24.4/g) and kidney at 21.5/g [6]. Those two rankings are not contradictory in principle — one is mass, the other particle count — but neither study measured both metrics in the same samples, so the discrepancy cannot be resolved with current data.

### 3. Methods: what each can and cannot establish

Py-GC/MS is the method at the center of the dispute. It is robust, high-throughput, and quantifies polymer mass, but it is destructive and yields no size or shape information [13]. More importantly for brain, a 2025 assessment concluded that Py-GC/MS is currently not a suitable method for polyethylene or PVC in biological matrices because of persistent interferences and nonspecific pyrolysis products [14]. A critical review of the technique similarly flags the absence of standardized sample-preparation and calibration protocols and the lack of internal standards as the prime obstacles to comparability [15].

The lipid interference is not hypothetical. In the Rauert validation work, pure coconut oil, glyceryl trioleate and sunflower oil each generated apparent polyethylene signals ranging from 10 to 2,960 µg per injection, against a consistent 4.6–5.3 µg per injection for a polyethylene-only standard [3]. The interference also distorted the deuterated polystyrene internal-standard confirmation ion (m/z 82), raising ion ratios 70–250% above calibration values, and the supposedly selective C21 diene marker was still present in lipid samples near detection limits — meaning alternative marker ions are not a universal fix [3]. Raising thermal-desorption temperature to 380–400 °C removed coconut-oil interference but not the unsaturated oils, and only a hexane:isopropanol wash cleared two of the three lipid types [3].

Infrared and Raman methods have the opposite profile: they identify polymer type and particle morphology but miss the smallest particles and are vulnerable to spectral interference from proteins and other organic matter [16]. Optical photothermal infrared microscopy reaches roughly 200–800 nm resolution, but its signal amplitude is generally not used for direct quantitation because the signal depends on density, heat capacity, thermal conductivity and particle size.

The practical consequence is that no single method can both quantify mass and confirm polymer identity at the nanoscale in a lipid-rich tissue. That is why a 30-plus-author framework paper led by Imperial College London and the University of Queensland recommends requiring at least two of the most powerful independent methods on the same sample before a detection is treated as definitive [17].

### 4. Contamination control: the sharpest methodological gap

The Matters Arising critique is specific: no procedural blanks, no field blanks, no matrix-contamination assessment, no validated digestion or recovery protocol, no polymer-spiking experiments, no reported limits of detection or quantification, and no identification-accuracy validation [2]. It also notes that samples were collected across different years and locations, raising uncertainty about harmonized contamination-minimizing protocols [2].

The digestion point is quantitative. Nihart et al. used 10% KOH at 40 °C for 72 hours, and their own supplementary data reportedly show digestion efficiencies below 90% for several brain samples [2]. Residual undigested lipid in a matrix that is roughly 60% fat by dry weight is precisely the condition under which the Rauert interference operates [2][3].

By contrast, the 2026 Nature Health study describes a more complete control set: airborne controls during post-mortem collection that read zero by both LDIR and Py-GC/MS, intraoperative blanks from specimen bottles, syringes, surgical materials and gloved-finger rinsate, and tests showing no correlation between plastic burden and infusion volume (P = 0.081) or surgical duration (P = 0.65) [5]. That is the standard the field is moving toward, and it is a meaningful contrast with the earlier work.

The Nihart authors have responded. A Reply was published in Nature Medicine (31:4036–4037), but only its metadata and reference list were accessible; the substantive defense of blanks, marker ions and digestion efficiency could not be verified. Separately, the senior author confirmed that three sets of duplicated ATR-FTIR supplement images were a "clerical" error, and outside researchers quoted in coverage said the duplications likely do not alter the conclusions because the images were illustrative [4].

### 5. Polymer composition: environmental signal or laboratory artifact?

The composition data cut both ways. Polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC and styrene-butadiene rubber all increased from 2016 to 2024 in liver and brain, and polyethylene, polypropylene and PVC together account for about 90% of mass in many samples [1]. A temporal trend tracking real-world production would be a strong argument for genuine environmental signal.

But the critique counters that polyethylene dominance was uniform across all organs, timepoints and individuals, unlike the greater polymer diversity typical of environmental exposure studies [2]. If true environmental exposure drove the signal, one would expect more heterogeneity. The lipid-mimicry mechanism predicts exactly the observed pattern: a polyethylene signal that scales with the fat content of the tissue, which is highest in brain.

The olfactory bulb data offer a useful contrast point. There, polypropylene dominated at 43.8%, with polyamide, nylon and polyethylene vinyl acetate also present [10] — a more diverse profile than the cortex findings. That difference could reflect genuine regional variation, different exposure routes, or simply different analytical methods; the evidence does not distinguish among these.

### 6. Independent replication: partial, and not of the contested quantity

The Nihart study did include one cross-laboratory check: five 2016 brain samples reanalyzed by Py-GC/MS at Oklahoma State University gave consistent values (P = 0.49), with about 25% within-sample coefficient of variation at both labs [1][12]. That is a genuine inter-laboratory comparison, but it tests reproducibility of the same method on the same samples, not the validity of the method against an independent technique.

The 2026 Nature Health study is the closest thing to independent confirmation of presence. A separate group, using LDIR, Py-GC/MS, nanoscale Py-GC/MS, O-PTIR and SEM, found micro/nanoplastics in 99.4% of diseased samples and 100% of healthy samples, with peritumoural tissue higher than healthy tissue [5]. Cross-method agreement was good but not perfect: of 18 particles compared by LDIR and O-PTIR, two disagreed [5]. Crucially, the absolute concentrations are paywalled, so it is not yet possible to say whether this group measured a burden anywhere near 3,000–5,000 µg/g or something far lower.

The interlaboratory comparison data explain why this matters. Even with homogeneous reference tablets of known polymer content, 56 laboratories produced reproducibility standard deviations of 46–129%, with three labs at about 4% relative standard deviation and three others above 100% [8]. If that spread applies to a matrix as difficult as brain, a single laboratory's absolute values carry wide uncertainty regardless of internal consistency.

### 7. What would settle it

The framework paper's standard is the clearest benchmark: at least two of the most powerful independent methods applied to the same sample, documented contamination controls, and explicit reporting of confidence levels for each detected particle [17]. Applied to brain, that would mean, at minimum, quantitative spectroscopic imaging on intact cortical sections (which preserves spatial and size information) alongside a mass-based method with spike recovery and matrix-matched calibration in lipid-rich tissue, plus procedural blanks carried through the entire workflow.

The Rauert work suggests a concrete first step: re-running archived brain samples with enzymatic digestion and solvent washes before pyrolysis, and comparing apparent polyethylene before and after. If the brain signal is largely lipid-derived, it should collapse by a large factor, as it did in food matrices [3]. If it survives, the case for genuine high-mass burden strengthens considerably.

## Risks and Counterpoints

The strongest counterargument to this report's skepticism is that the Nihart findings have internal coherence that a pure artifact would struggle to produce: a dose-response-like temporal trend, a plausible tissue ranking by lipid content and perfusion, and consistency across two laboratories using the same method [1][12]. The authors have also defended their work publicly, and the duplicated images were acknowledged as clerical rather than substantive [4].

A second counterpoint is that the lipid-interference evidence comes from food matrices, not brain. The 100–450-fold inflation factor was measured in milk, egg and lamb [3]; brain lipid composition differs, and the transfer of that factor is an inference, not a measurement. This report treats it as a plausible mechanism of unknown magnitude, not a demonstrated correction.

A third is that the critical literature is itself uneven. The Matters Arising is peer-reviewed and specific [2], but much of the public criticism comes from journalism and institutional press releases [4][17][14], and one critical review is a single-author piece that relies on secondhand summaries of a German regulatory statement [18]. The critique is well-founded on the analytical chemistry, but not every voice in the debate carries equal evidentiary weight.

Finally, the independent detection studies cut against a purely dismissive reading. If the brain signal were entirely contamination or lipid artifact, one would not expect a separate group using five methods and airborne controls reading zero to find particles in 100% of healthy samples [5]. The most defensible position is that polymers are present, and that the reported masses are probably too high by an unknown factor.

## Open Questions

Several gaps would materially change the answer if resolved. The absolute concentrations from the 2026 Nature Health study are paywalled; if that group reports values in the tens or low hundreds of µg/g rather than thousands, the Nihart magnitudes would be effectively refuted without contradicting the presence finding [5]. The full text of the authors' Reply would show whether they have addressed the marker-ion and digestion-efficiency points with data. A brain-specific replication of the Rauert enzymatic-digestion comparison would convert the lipid-interference argument from analogy into measurement [3]. And the dementia signal — the 26,076 µg/g median in 12 decedents [1] — has no independent check in the retrieved literature, so it should be treated as an unconfirmed observation rather than an established association.
